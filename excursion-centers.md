# Strict growth at small corank for every size

Let $n$ be the row count, $\delta=0$ for the odd-part staircase and $\delta=1$ for the even-part staircase. Set $m=2n+\delta$, $s=m^{3/2}$, $D=\lfloor m^2/4\rfloor$, and $b_k=a_{D-k}$. For both families,
$$ b_{k+1}>b_k \qquad (n\ge2,\quad 0\le k\le s/70). \tag{1} $$
Here and below coefficient indices are integers. Thus a possible failure of unimodality at any outstanding size can be sought on the peak scale between $s/70$ and $3s/2$, allowing one adjacent index at the boundaries. The [first-passage inequality](rising-window.md) and [lower-half inequality](monotonicity.md#1-lower-half-for-all-sizes) handle the other end. At $n=1$ both sequences consist entirely of ones.

There is also an all-size comparison which follows directly from the ordinary type-A diagonal coinvariant theorem. Form the increasing multiset of nonnegative integers in which degree $j$ occurs
$$ w_\delta(j)=\left\lfloor\frac{(j+2)^2}{4}\right\rfloor
       +{\bf1}_{\{j=2+2\delta+4r,\ r\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\}} $$
times. Let $S_\delta(n)$ be the sum of its $n$ smallest entries. Then
$$ b_i<b_j\qquad (0\le i<j\le D,\quad i+j\le S_\delta(n)). \tag{2} $$
In particular the sequence increases strictly from zero corank through $\lceil S_\delta(n)/2\rceil$. This endpoint is $\tfrac38\,12^{1/3} n^{4/3}+O(n)$. For example, at $n=2049$ it is 20,284 for the odd-part staircase and 20,290 for the even-part staircase. Equation (1) results from overlapping (2) with a lattice saddle estimate. The [verifier](verify_excursion_centers.py) checks the overlap, the numerical constants of that estimate, and small coefficient rows independently.

## Ordinary excursions and two-variable characters

Write $C_l(q)$ for the area polynomial of nonnegative walks of $2l$ signs returning to zero. Its area is the usual Dyck area. In fact $\sum_t\lfloor H_t/2\rfloor$ equals the sum of heights immediately before up-steps. Exactly $l$ heights are odd, and summing $H_t^2-H_{t-1}^2=2(H_t-H_{t-1})H_{t-1}+1$ gives the identity.

We use the following published type-A results. Haiman identifies the bigraded Hilbert series of the sign component of the diagonal coinvariant ring on $l$ pairs of variables with the $q,t$-Catalan polynomial. Garsia and Haiman identify its specialization at $t=1$ with $C_l(q)$. These results apply to each ordinary excursion in the decomposition below. [Haiman, *Vanishing theorems and character formulas*, pp. 2, 18–20](https://math.berkeley.edu/~mhaiman/ftp/vanishing/van.pdf) · [Garsia–Haiman, *A remarkable $q,t$-Catalan sequence and $q$-Lagrange inversion*](https://math.berkeley.edu/~mhaiman/ftp/remarkable-qtcat/qtcat.pdf)

Let $E(l)$ be the sum of the $l$ smallest degrees $a+b$ for distinct $(a,b)\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^2$, and $E(0)=0$. **The polynomial $C_l$ is a sum of interval polynomials $q^a+\cdots+q^b$ with $a+b\ge E(l)$, and some interval attains equality.**

To prove this, simultaneously act on each pair $(x_i,y_i)$ by $GL_2$. This commutes with permutations of the pairs and preserves the ideal generated by positive-degree invariants. The alternating part of the quotient in each total degree $d$ is a polynomial $GL_2$-module. In characteristic zero its irreducibles have the form $\det^v\otimes\operatorname{Sym}^{u-v}$, $u\ge v\ge0$, $u+v=d$. At the diagonal matrix with entries $q,1$ their characters are the intervals $q^v+\cdots+q^u$.

Antisymmetrizing monomials spans the alternating polynomials. Repetition of an exponent pair makes an antisymmetrization vanish, so no total degree below $E(l)$ occurs. Choosing $l$ distinct pairs of least total cost gives a nonzero determinant of degree $E(l)$. Its class survives in the quotient. Indeed, antisymmetrizing a multiple of a positive-degree invariant in that degree would require an alternating multiplier in smaller degree. This proves the interval assertion, including $C_0=1$.

For the weaker endpoint bound needed by the staircase induction, Lean also gives a direct calculation with the area/dinv formula. In the area word of a Dyck path of semilength $l$, label each entry $a$ by $(a,b)$ with $b$ its number of later equal entries. These $l$ pairs are distinct. The sum of their first coordinates is area, and the sum of their second coordinates counts only equal-height inversions. Hence area plus dinv is at least $E(l)$. Each nonempty total-degree slice therefore has degree at least the required cutoff. Its symmetry and unimodality give the reflected inequalities for that slice, and summing over the slices proves them for $C_l(q)$. The identification of the area/dinv enumerator with the diagonal harmonic character is the published input to this argument. Lean checks the degree bound and summation, with the symmetry and unimodality of the slices as an explicit hypothesis. [Area/dinv and character formulas, equations (50)–(51)](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0310424) · [Lean proof](CentralUnimodality/DyckDiagonalSlices.lean)

### Reflected dimensions from raising and lowering

There is a direct operator proof of the inequality needed in the induction. In total degree $d$, let $V_k$ be the subspace of first degree $k$. Suppose $E$ raises this degree by one, $F$ lowers it by one, $FV_0=0$, and $(EF-FE)v=(2k-d)v$ on $V_k$. Then $E^{j-i}:V_i\to V_j$ is injective whenever $i\le j$ and $i+j\le d$. The statement holds over the rationals for all $d$.

Indeed, commuting $F$ through $s+1$ copies of $E$ gives, for $v\in V_k$,
$$
 E^{s+1}Fv=FE^{s+1}v+(s+1)(2k-d+s)E^s v.
$$
Suppose $2k+s<d$ and $E^{s+1}v=0$. The identity with $s+1$ in place of $s$ gives $E^{s+2}Fv=0$. Induction on $k$ gives $E^{s+1}Fv=0$ since $Fv\in V_{k-1}$, with $Fv=0$ at $k=0$. The displayed scalar is nonzero, so $E^s v=0$. Repeating this implication proves injectivity. Summing the resulting dimension inequalities over the total degrees above the cutoff gives the reflected coefficients of the univariate polynomial. Lean checks this argument and its use in the staircase induction. [Operator and dimension theorems](CentralUnimodality/DyckWeightOperators.lean)

For the diagonal coinvariants the intended operators are $E=\sum_i x_i\partial_{y_i}$ and $F=\sum_i y_i\partial_{x_i}$. The formal theorem still requires a realization of the graded spaces, their degree cutoff, and the identification of the summed dimensions with $[q^k]C_l(q)$. The specialization at $t=1$ in the cited type A result supplies the univariate identification in the written proof.

## Unmatched up-steps and the minimum sum of interval endpoints

Match each down-step of a surviving walk with the last available up-step. If its final height is $e=2u+\delta$, its $e$ unmatched up-steps separate ordinary excursions at heights $i=0,\ldots,e$. Let their semilengths be $l_i$. Matched pairs cannot enclose an unmatched step, and inserting any excursions among these single up-steps recovers a unique walk. Here $\sum_i l_i=n-u$. The polynomial for fixed $u,(l_i)$ is
$$
 q^J\prod_{i=0}^{2u+\delta} C_{l_i}(q),\qquad
 J=u(u+\delta)+\sum_i i\,l_i . \tag{3}
$$
The unmatched steps contribute $\sum_{b=1}^e\lfloor b/2\rfloor=\lfloor e^2/4\rfloor$. Translating an excursion by $i$ adds $i\,l_i$, since its vertex heights have equally many of each parity.

A product of two intervals is a sum of intervals with center the sum of their centers. For $I_d=1+\cdots+q^d$, use $I_d I_e=\sum_{v=0}^{\min(d,e)}q^v I_{d+e-2v}$. Thus the minimum sum of the two interval endpoints in (3) is
$$ 2u(u+\delta)+\sum_i(2i\,l_i+E(l_i)). \tag{4} $$
It is attained by taking a least-degree irreducible in each factor.

The minimum of (4) over all the data in (3) equals $S_\delta(n)$. To see this, choose $l_i$ distinct integer pairs $(a,b)$ in layer $i$, each of cost $2i+a+b$. Use the first $u$ additional entries of costs $4v-2+2\delta$, $v=1,2,\ldots$. Their sum is $2u(u+\delta)$. Every such choice uses $n$ entries. If all layers $i\ge0$ are allowed, the multiplicity of cost $j$ in the layers is $\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor j/2\rfloor}(j-2i+1)=\lfloor(j+2)^2/4\rfloor$. Including the additional entries gives the stated multiset.

Conversely select its $n$ cheapest entries, ordering an additional entry first at each tie. The additional entries selected form an initial segment of length $u$. The next one costs $4u+2+2\delta$, exactly the smallest cost in the first excluded layer $i=2u+\delta+1$. Thus every selected triple occurs in a permitted layer. The choice realizes (4), proving the minimum formula.

For any interval $[a,b]$ with $a+b\ge S_\delta(n)$, if $i<j$ with $i+j\le S_\delta(n)$ and $i\in[a,b]$, then also $j\in[a,b]$. This gives the weak comparison for each summand. For strictness every $j=0,\ldots,D$ occurs as the initial exponent $J$ of some class (3), with positive coefficient there. At fixed $u$, distributing $n-u$ units among the levels $0,\ldots,2u+\delta$ attains every integer between $0$ and $(2u+\delta)(n-u)$ in the weighted sum. The attainable offsets therefore run from $u(u+\delta)$ to $(2u+\delta)n-u^2$. There is no gap at the next $u$: the upper endpoint plus one minus the next lower endpoint is $(2u+\delta)(n-u-1)$ for $u<n$. The first interval starts at zero and the last ends at $D=n(n+\delta)$. A class with $J=j$ has zero coefficient at $i<j$, proving (2).

The cumulative multiplicity through $t$ is $t^3/12+O(t^2)$ and is at most $(t+4)^3/12$ for integer $t\ge0$. For the inequality bound $\sum_{j=0}^t (j+2)^2/4$ by the integral of $(x+2)^2/4$ from $0$ to $t+1$, then add $t+1$. Consequently the $j$th cheapest degree is at least $(12j)^{1/3}-4$. Integrating the cube root up to $n$ yields
$$ S_\delta(n)/2\ \ge\ \tfrac38\,12^{1/3} n^{4/3}-2n . \tag{5} $$
The cumulative asymptotic gives the claimed leading constant.

## The lattice saddle at height 220

We give the analytic changes needed to prove (1) at every size. Use the symmetric two-step transfer $K_\beta$ for the tilt $e^{-\beta k}$, $p(\beta)=\log\|K_\beta\|$, $H=\beta^{-1/3}$, and $N=n/H^2$. **At every integer saddle $k=-np'(\beta)$ with $H\ge220$ and $N\ge1450$, both parities satisfy $b_{k+1}>b_k$.** The [transfer-power proof](airy-powers.md#inverting-at-the-integer-saddle) establishes this at $H\ge10000$. We verify its frequency estimates and boundary normalization at the smaller height. Write $d=1/H$, and let $2.337<a<2.339$ be the continuum Airy ground energy as [bracketed in the previous proof](variation-overlap.md#a-uniform-interval-in-corank).

**Fourier powers.** Put $t=1+iy$, $|y|\le1$, $E=at^{2/3}$, $V=tx$, $L=E^2/30$. Take the unit continuum Airy eigenfunction $f$ and the corrected sample
$$ U=f+d^2F,\qquad F=(2tx/15)f+(tx^2-2Ex)f'/30,\qquad
 \zeta=\exp(3td^3/4-(E+Ld^2)d^2/2). $$
The [grid cancellation](airy-grid.md#sampling-the-half-line-eigenfunction) through degree four is an identity in $t$. On the entire indicated segment $\Re t=1$, $|t|<1.42$, $|E|<3$ and $\Re E,\Re L>0$. Using these bounds in the grid Taylor remainders gives a single-step residual at most $290e^{3d^3/4}d^6$. In detail, apply the weighted moment recursion there with denominator $\Re t=1$ and upper energy 3. In the residual for $Bf$ use the same seven terms, with $T=1.42$. In the residual for $BF$ the coefficient of $\|F'\|$ becomes $.36$ since $T/4<.36$. The bound $.07d^6$ on the hyperbolic remainders follows from $T^2e^{Td^3/4}/32<.07$. The scalar remainder bounds are still $1$ and $1.3$ in the same units as in that proof. The verifier evaluates all these norms, including the grid corrections. The even-height norm is at least $(1-2d\sqrt3)/\sqrt2-d^2\|F\|_d$. Squaring costs at most two, by real domination and $|\zeta|\le1$.

Set $\lambda=e^{p(\beta)}$, $A=K_{t\beta}/\lambda$. For the conjugate normalized even sample $v$ the resulting bounds are
$$
 \|A^*v-\overline{\nu}v\|\le 900d^6,\qquad
 \left|-d^{-2}\log|\nu|-a(\Re(t^{2/3})-1)\right|\le .60d^2,\qquad \nu=\zeta^2/\lambda .
$$
For the second inequality the quadratic correction costs at most $a^2(1+1.6)d^2/30$ and the real grid remainder costs $2000d^4$. The linear correction cancels. These two bounds suffice for the [power contraction outside the perturbation disk](airy-powers.md#a-power-bound-outside-the-perturbation-disk). That argument compares the approximate left eigenvector to the leading singular direction using the real gap $1.48d^2$, then iterates in the norm $\max(\|w\|,B|\langle v,w\rangle|)$. In its scalar tests substitute $\Delta=900d^4$ and energy error $.60d^2$. The verifier checks both bands at $d=1/220$. The resulting power bounds are unchanged, namely $2e^{-.173j d^2 y^2}$ on $.08\le |y|\le.2$ and $4e^{-.12j d^2 y^2}$ on $.2\le |y|\le1$ after $j$ pairs.

**Real boundary sums.** The principal amplitude satisfies $.99 I/H<c_\delta(\beta)<1.01I/H$ at all $H\ge210$, where $I=\int_0^\infty f>1/2$ for the positive real unit ground $f$. Keep the correction $F$ when estimating the terminal sum. For a mesh of width $h$, the integrated midpoint and trapezoidal errors are both at most $h^2\|g''\|_1/8$. On each cell the integral remainder kernel has absolute value at most $h^2/8$ and the bounds sum. For a decaying $g$ vanishing at zero the full right-end sum equals the trapezoidal sum. Apply this first to $g=f^2$: the squared grid norm differs from one by at most $d^2(2a+2\|f''\|_2)/8<3d^2$. Since the real grid bound gives $\|F\|_d<4$, the corrected sample norm $s_U$ lies within $7d^2$ of one. As in the [real amplitude proof](airy-powers.md#real-boundary-amplitude), the normalized corrected sample differs from the true full unit ground $u$ by at most $400d^4$ in sequence norm. Indeed the perpendicular spectrum is at least $.73d^2$ from the trial eigenvalue and the single-step residual at real tilt is at most $201d^6$ before normalization.

Write $S_\delta^{\rm end}=\sum_{j\equiv\delta\ (2)}u_j e^{-\beta\lfloor j/2\rfloor/2}$. The error just obtained, multiplied by the terminal norm and $2\sqrt d$, costs at most $1600d^3$. For the uncorrected function $f$ use the midpoint or trapezoidal error on the parity mesh of width $2d$. The terminal weight change costs at most $(d^2/4)(21+106d)$ by the previous real amplitude proof. For the correction bound the terminal sum after scaling by $d^2(\|F\|_1+2d\|F'\|_1)$, using the rectangular Riemann bound on $|F|$. The real moment recursion with $a<2.34$ gives $\|f''\|_1<26$, $\|F\|_1<12$, $\|F'\|_1<35$, since $\|g\|_1\le\|(1+x)g\|_2$. Thus, with $A_d=d^2(13+(21+106d)/4+12+70d)$,
$$ \left|2\sqrt d S_\delta^{\rm end}/I-1\right|
       \le (2A_d+7d^2)/(1-7d^2)+3200d^3 < .003 . $$
The boundary identity gives $2(1-e^{-\beta/2})\lambda\le u_0^2\le\beta$ and $c_\delta=2u_0(\sqrt\lambda)^\delta S_\delta^{\rm end}$. With $\lambda\ge1-2.60d^2$ these give the claimed amplitude interval.

**Signed inversion.** At $H\ge220$ the real shifts $t=1\pm.08$ have height at least 210. The amplitude ratio is thus below $1.06$, giving the previous bound $205$ for the second moment of the shorter walk in tilt units. On $|t-1|\le.08$ the cubic Cauchy bound from the analytic grid expansion is below $H^7$, using
$$ \frac{8\cdot2.339}{27(.92)^3}
       +\frac{8\cdot.264}{27H^2(.92)^3}
       +\frac{12000}{H^4(.04)^3}<1. $$
For the band $4.5\le |y|\le45$ the spacing condition $12\cdot45/H<\pi$ holds, and the real energy comparison for the Hölder block is valid by the [grid proof at $H\ge110$](airy-grid.md#boundary-factors-in-fourier-inversion). The end blocks, local complementary term, multiple-frequency bounds and the full-period tail require no further change. In particular $H^3/64>10^4$. Applying the [integer-saddle inversion](airy-powers.md#inverting-at-the-integer-saddle) with the two power bounds above and the real moment bound therefore gives the same positive margin $>.20$ at $N\ge1450$. The verifier reruns its inequalities at $H=220$ with the bracketed energy. This proves the sign at the stated saddles.

## Overlap for every row count

For $2\le n<64$ the integer formula for $S_\delta$ gives $(70\lceil S_\delta(n)/2\rceil)^2>(2n+\delta)^3$ for both parities. Suppose $64\le n\le70\,000\,000$. Writing $y=n^{1/3}$, the concave function $.858y-(2.016y)^{3/2}/70$ exceeds $2+1/64$ at both endpoints, as the verifier checks. Here $m\le2.016n$ and $\tfrac38\,12^{1/3}>.858$. By (5), $S_\delta(n)/2>s/70+1$. These sizes satisfy (1) by (2).

For larger $n$ it remains to treat $k\ge\lceil S_\delta(n)/2\rceil$, $k\le s/70$. Now $k/n\ge .858 n^{1/3}-2>.858\cdot412-2$. The [slope bijection](monotonicity.md#3-decrease-near-the-top) selects a positive $\beta$ with $k=-np'(\beta)$. At $H=220$ the real grid derivative satisfies
$$ -p'(\beta)/H \le 2a/3 + 4a^2/(90H^2)+2000/(.12 H^4). $$
This follows by differentiating the explicit terms and applying Cauchy's inequality to the grid error on the $.12$ disk. The resulting upper bound for $-p'$ is smaller than $.858\cdot412-2$. Strict monotonicity of the slope in $H$ puts the selected height above 220. There $k/n>(2a/3-.016)H$ by the grid slope bound. Hence
$$ N>70^2(2\cdot2.337/3-.016)^2/(2.001)^3 >1450 $$
using $m/n<2.001$. The saddle result completes (1).

The individual inequalities in (1) hold for both families at every $n\ge2$. They leave the signs between the two analytic tails undecided for $2049\le n<10^{17}$. The finite evidence in the verifier consists of a separate minimum-cost calculation and direct enumeration through $n=12$, a check of (2) on the full rows through $n=40$, and word-by-word checks of (3) through length 14.
